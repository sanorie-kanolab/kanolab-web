Inclusive Science Communication and STEAM Education

Science should not belong only to people who are already interested in it.

Science communication and STEAM (Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts/Liberal Arts, and Mathematics) education can appear successful when we look only at the people who attended. Those who never encountered the opportunity, did not see it as relevant, could not meet the participation conditions, or faced language and accessibility barriers are easily left outside evaluation.

The Kano Lab asks not only “Who participated?” but also “Who did not—and what made participation difficult?” We shift attention away from perceived deficits in learners or citizens and towards the design of education, events, institutions and media.

1｜Including people with potential or low interest in science and technology

Science events and policy dialogues often attract people who are already highly interested in science. We have studied engagement with science and policy, intentions to participate, actual audiences and the potential of cultural or everyday-life entry points that do not foreground science.

“Potentially interest” or “Low interest” is not treated simply as an individual trait. Communication, place, timing, cost, framing and the degree to which people feel safe and welcome all shape participation.

2｜Women in STEM

We study masculine public images of physics and mathematics, teachers’ unconscious gender bias, and policies and institutional conditions that shape educational choices.

Our aim is not to ask women and girls to adapt to an unchanged STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) culture. We examine who is perceived as “suited” to STEM and how classroom and social expectations might be transformed so that a wider range of people can imagine their own futures.

In this area, Kano participates in collaborative research led by Professor Hiromi Yokoyama at the University of Tokyo. Building on a JST-RISTEX project launched in 2017, the team continues this work through the JSPS KAKENHI project “Understanding the STEM Equality Paradox” (2023–2027), examining how social norms, disciplinary images, family and school expectations, and institutional conditions shape women’s progression into STEM fields.

3｜Deaf and hard-of-hearing children and their families

We have co-designed STEM learning in which deaf and hard-of-hearing children and their families can investigate together at home and online. Sign language, text, photographs, diagrams, physical materials and tactile activities provide multiple routes to information and participation.

Accessibility is not an accommodation added after a resource has been completed. It is a research question that shapes the activity, explanation, dialogue, assessment and process of co-design from the beginning.

4｜Culturally relevant STEAM education

Learners’ family knowledge, local culture, language, food, making practices and relationships with nature are educational resources. Using Kakishibu, Kyoto vegetables, tea and wagashi, we connect scientific observation, comparison and experimentation with history, culture, design and sustainability.

Culture is not decoration added to a science lesson. It can expand what counts as a meaningful question and as valuable knowledge.

Key research questions

  • How can science engagement create meaningful entry points for people with low interest in science and technology?
  • What gaps exist between intention to participate and actual audiences?
  • How do gendered images of STEM and teacher expectations shape educational choices?
  • What conditions allow deaf and hard-of-hearing children and family members to participate as equal co-investigators?
  • How does culturally relevant STEAM education affect learners’ ideas and environmental imagination?
  • How should inclusion be evaluated through experience, voice, decision-making and ownership—not attendance alone?

Methods

We combine audience surveys, questionnaires, interviews, observation, conversation and video analysis, co-design, resource development, classroom and workshop evaluation, and international comparison. Outputs may include papers, educational resources, facilitation guidance, accessibility principles and policy recommendations.

Research opportunities

We welcome proposals from education, science communication, gender studies, disability studies, sociology, psychology, design, language, accessibility and policy. Proposals should value collaboration with participants and practitioners and include a plan for returning benefits and findings.

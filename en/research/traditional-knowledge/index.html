Traditional and Indigenous Knowledge for a Sustainable Society

Reconsidering inherited knowledge as a resource for sustainable futures.

Traditional knowledge is not a catalogue of old techniques and materials. It is a knowledge system embedded in knowledge holders, histories, languages, livelihoods, values and relationships with nature.

The Kano Lab works primarily with Japanese traditional knowledge while learning from international discussions of Indigenous knowledge. We do not treat knowledge as a detachable resource. Our research asks whose knowledge is involved, who represents it, who decides how it is used, and how benefits and recognition are shared.

Central case｜Kakishibu

Kakishibu is a traditional Japanese material made by fermenting and ageing the juice of astringent persimmons. It has been applied to paper, textiles, wood, fishing tools and architecture. Its significance lies not only in water resistance, deodorising, antimicrobial or insect-repellent functions, but also in the relationships among local environments, seasons, production skills and users’ experience.

Our work connects:

  • the history of Kakishibu and Japanese views of nature;
  • fermentation, ageing, application and material function;
  • the practical knowledge of producers, craftspeople and users;
  • alternatives to plastic, disposability and fossil-based materials;
  • STEAM (Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts/Liberal Arts, and Mathematics) education in which children design sustainable inventions;
  • green innovation that respects local cultures; and
  • international comparison among learners and researchers.

Rather than simply “rediscovering” Kakishibu as a new material, we ask what is protected or transformed when traditional knowledge enters contemporary innovation—and who benefits.

Flagship case 2｜Tea

Tea brings together botany, cultivation, processing, oxidation, extraction, aroma, astringency, colour, taste, utensils, social practices, trade and community memory. In our learning design, comparison is not a test with one correct answer. Learners express sensory differences through speech, sign, images and other forms, and use those differences to formulate questions.

The “Tea Workshop—Tastes, Traditions and Shared Intangible Cultural Heritage” involved high-school students and deaf and hard-of-hearing primary-school students. It connected language, biology, chemistry, history, geography and home economics, using tea to explore both diversity and shared cultural connections across East Asia. The project is featured in a UNESCO report and official story.

Other cultural resources explored in earlier work

Kyoto vegetables｜Varieties, taste and local narratives

Work with Kyoto vegetables has connected crop diversity, cultivation, aroma, taste, food practices and regional history with scientific inquiry. The aim is not to explain a vegetable only through its components, but to ask why it has been cultivated in a particular place and how value has been sustained.

Wagashi｜Craft knowledge and kitchen science

Wagashi brings together ingredients, heating, cooling, moisture, colour, form and seasonality. Respecting the knowledge of craftspeople, learners investigate what underpins their know-how and how they pass their skills from generation to generation.

Connection with the UNESCO Chair

The UNESCO Chair on STEAM Education for Braiding Traditional Cultural Resources with Science begins with Kakishibu and tea and extends to examples such as rice cultivation and sake brewing, and traditional architecture. Knowledge holders, schools, researchers and international partners co-design STEAM learning, teacher development, research and mobility related to climate action, water management and disaster resilience.

Knowledge ethics and equitable collaboration

Research involving traditional or Indigenous knowledge should make the following explicit:

  • knowledge holders and communities can participate in decisions about questions and methods;
  • sources, contributions, authorship, images and stories are appropriately attributed;
  • knowledge that may be made public is distinguished from knowledge that should remain restricted;
  • data, resources, commercialisation, intellectual property and benefit sharing are discussed in advance;
  • findings are returned in forms and languages that communities can use; and
  • cultures are not frozen in the name of preservation; communities retain the right to change.

Research opportunities

We welcome proposals from traditional-material studies, local culture, sustainability education, science and technology studies, design, materials, food studies, agriculture and environmental policy. Proposals should include plans for relationships with knowledge holders, research ethics and the sharing of outcomes.
